Chapter 1

Limitations of Classical Computing

1.1 Introduction

Classical computers have fundamentally reshaped modern human civilization. From personal microprocessors to giant cloud supercomputers, all classical hardware relies on binary bits.

ℹ️
Classical Binary Bit Principle A classical bit is a deterministic physical state (such as electrical voltage levels) that exists strictly in one of two discrete states at any given moment: $0$ (OFF) or $1$ (ON).

Even though modern silicon processors contain over 50 billion micro-transistors operating at gigahertz speeds, certain classes of mathematical, chemical, and computational problems remain impossible or impractical for classical architectures regardless of scale.

1.2 How Classical Computers Process Information

Classical computing operates sequentially or via multi-core parallel execution based on Boolean logic gates (AND, OR, NOT, NAND, XOR). Information representation follows strict scaling:

💻
1 Bit

Single binary state: 0 or 1.

💾
1 Byte (8 Bits)

256 possible discrete states ($2^8$), but holds only 1 state at a time.

🗃
32-Bit Register

$4.29 imes 10^9$ possible states ($2^{32}$), holding exactly 1 number at a time.

💽
64-Bit Register

$1.84 imes 10^{19}$ states ($2^{64}$), evaluated one sequence at a time.

1.3 The Breakdown of Moore's Law

In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors packed onto a microchip doubles roughly every two years, driving logarithmic drops in computing cost and exponential gains in processor speed.

⚠️
The Physical Wall of Silicon As silicon gate lengths shrink down to 3nm and 2nm scales (approaching atomic sizes of ~0.2nm per atom), classical physics collapses due to Quantum Tunneling. Electrons leak across thin insulating barriers, producing immense heat and computational corruption.

Physical Bottlenecks Ending Moore's Law

  • Quantum Electron Leakage: Electrons spontaneously tunnel across nanometer-scale gate junctions.
  • Thermal Dissipation Wall: Excessive power density produces heat impossible to cool economically.
  • Economic Lithography Limits: EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) lithography costs billions per fabrication plant.

Exponential Combinatorial Explosion Examples

  1. Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP): Finding the shortest route visiting $N$ cities:
    • 5 Cities → 120 possible routes
    • 10 Cities → 3.6 million routes
    • 20 Cities → $2.4 imes 10^{18}$ routes (Classical supercomputer takes years)
    • 50 Cities → $3 imes 10^{64}$ routes (Exceeds total atoms in the observable universe!)
  2. Simulating Molecular Chemistry (Feynman's Insight):

    As Nobel Laureate Richard Feynman noted in 1981: "Nature isn't classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical!"

    Simulating the caffeine molecule ($C_8H_{10}N_4O_2$) requires $10^{48}$ classical bits. Simulating a simple penicillin molecule requires more bits than atoms in the universe!

🧠 Chapter 1 Key Takeaways

  • Classical computing relies on deterministic bits ($0$ or $1$) and faces physical quantum tunneling limits.
  • Combinatorial optimization and molecular chemistry scale exponentially, making classical solutions intractable.
Chapter 2

Physics Foundations of Quantum Computing

2.1 Wave-Particle Duality & Energy Quantization

Quantum Mechanics emerged in the early 20th century through breakthroughs by Max Planck, Albert Einstein, Niels Bohr, and Louis de Broglie:

⚡
1. Quantization of Energy

Energy is emitted in discrete packets called quanta ($E = h u$, where $h$ is Planck's constant).

💫
2. Wave-Particle Duality

Subatomic particles (electrons, photons) exhibit both particle collisions and wave interference properties ($\lambda = rac{h}{p}$).

📏
3. Heisenberg Uncertainty

Simultaneous measurement of position $x$ and momentum $p$ is fundamentally constrained ($\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge rac{\hbar}{2}$).

2.2 The Measurement Problem & Wavefunction Collapse

In quantum physics, a closed physical system evolves deterministically according to the Schrödinger Wave Equation:

📝
Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation $$i\hbar rac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi(t) angle = \hat{H} |\Psi(t) angle$$ Where $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator representing total system energy, and $|\Psi(t) angle$ is the state vector.

However, when an observer measures a quantum state, the continuous wavefunction instantly collapses probabilistically into one definite eigenstate. This probabilistic collapse is governed by Born's Rule ($P(x) = |\psi(x)|^2$).

The Qubit & Mathematical Dirac Notation

3.1 Mathematical Representation of Qubits

While a classical bit stores either $0$ or $1$, a Quantum Bit (Qubit) is a two-level quantum system represented as a normalized unit vector in a 2-dimensional complex Hilbert space ($\mathbb{C}^2$).

🧠
Dirac Ket Notation (Computational Basis States) $$|0 angle = egin{bmatrix} 1 \ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad |1 angle = egin{bmatrix} 0 \ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

A qubit state $|\psi angle$ in superposition is expressed as:

⚖
Qubit Superposition State Equation $$|\psi angle = lpha |0 angle + eta |1 angle = egin{bmatrix} lpha \ eta \end{bmatrix}$$ Where $lpha, eta \in \mathbb{C}$ are complex probability amplitudes satisfying the Normalization Constraint: $$|lpha|^2 + |eta|^2 = 1$$
  • $|lpha|^2$ represents the exact probability of measuring the state $|0 angle$.
  • $|eta|^2$ represents the exact probability of measuring the state $|1 angle$.

3.2 Geometric Representation: The Bloch Sphere

Any arbitrary single-qubit state $|\psi angle$ can be mapped onto the surface of a 3D unit sphere called the Bloch Sphere using polar angle $ heta$ and azimuthal phase angle $\phi$:

🌍
Bloch Sphere Parametric Equation $$|\psi angle = \cos\left( rac{ heta}{2} ight) |0 angle + e^{i\phi} \sin\left( rac{ heta}{2} ight) |1 angle$$ Where $0 \le heta \le \pi$ and $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$.
⬆️
North Pole ($ heta = 0$)

Represents state $|0 angle$.

⬇️
South Pole ($ heta = \pi$)

Represents state $|1 angle$.

Ⅰ
Equator ($ heta = rac{\pi}{2}$)

Represents equal superposition states $|+ angle = rac{|0 angle + |1 angle}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $|- angle = rac{|0 angle - |1 angle}{\sqrt{2}}$.

4.1 Quantum Superposition

Superposition allows a quantum system to exist in a linear combination of all basis states simultaneously prior to measurement.

4.3 Quantum Entanglement ("Spooky Action at a Distance")

Entanglement occurs when multi-qubit states cannot be factored into independent single-qubit state vectors ($|\psi_{AB} angle eq |\psi_A angle \otimes |\psi_B angle$).

The Four Maximally Entangled Bell States

⚡
$$\Phi^+ = rac{|00 angle + |11 angle}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \Phi^- = rac{|00 angle - |11 angle}{\sqrt{2}}$$ $$\Psi^+ = rac{|01 angle + |10 angle}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \Psi^- = rac{|01 angle - |10 angle}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Measuring Qubit A instantaneously determines the physical outcome of Qubit B regardless of spatial distance!

Hands-On Python Code: Creating a Bell State Entanglement Circuit with Qiskit

Python 3.10 (Qiskit 1.0) 
# Complete Qiskit Entanglement (Bell State) Implementation
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram

# 1. Create a 2-Qubit, 2-Classical-Bit Quantum Circuit
qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)

# 2. Apply Hadamard Gate to Qubit 0 (Create Superposition)
qc.h(0)

# 3. Apply Controlled-NOT (CNOT) Gate (Entangle Qubit 0 & Qubit 1)
qc.cx(0, 1)

# 4. Measure both Qubits into Classical Registers
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])

# Simulate Quantum Statevector prior to measurement
state = Statevector.from_instruction(qc.remove_final_measurements(inplace=False))
print(f"Entangled Bell State Vector: {state}")
# Expected Output: (|00> + |11>) / sqrt(2)

print(qc.draw(output='text'))
