Module 4 – Why Quantum Computing?

Chapter 1: Limitations of Classical Computing

1.1 Introduction

Classical computers have transformed human civilization. From smartphones to supercomputers, almost every digital device works using classical bits .

A classical bit can only exist in one of two states:

  • 0
  • 1

Even though modern processors contain billions of transistors, there are certain problems that become impossible or impractical to solve using classical computers.

These limitations led scientists to develop an entirely new computing paradigm called Quantum Computing .

1.2 How Classical Computers Work

A classical computer processes information using:

  • Binary bits
  • Logic gates
  • Electrical signals
  • Transistors
Example

Bit = 0
Bit = 1
8 Bits = 1 Byte

Everything inside a computer—images, videos, games, AI models—is ultimately represented using combinations of 0s and 1s.

1.3 Moore's Law

In 1965, Gordon Moore observed that:

  • The number of transistors on a chip doubles approximately every two years.

This resulted in:

  • Faster processors
  • Smaller devices
  • Lower cost
  • More memory

However, Moore's Law is slowing down.

Reason:
  • Transistors are becoming extremely tiny (only a few nanometers).

At this scale:

  • Quantum effects begin appearing
  • Heat increases
  • Leakage current increases
  • Manufacturing becomes difficult

1.4 Major Limitations of Classical Computing

A. Processing Power

Some problems require enormous computational resources.

Example

Finding the best route among millions of possibilities.

Classical computers must check many possibilities one by one.

B. Exponential Growth

Many scientific problems grow exponentially.

Example

Traveling Salesman Problem

If cities increase:

  • 5 cities → 120 routes
  • 10 cities → 3.6 million routes
  • 20 cities → 2.4 × 10 18 routes

Impossible to check every route.

C. Simulation of Nature

Richard Feynman said:

Nature is quantum mechanical.

Atoms and molecules follow quantum physics.

Classical computers struggle to simulate:

  • Molecules
  • Chemical reactions
  • Protein folding
  • Quantum materials
D. Cryptography

Modern encryption:

  • RSA

Security depends upon factoring huge prime numbers.

Example

N = P × Q

Finding P and Q for large numbers is extremely difficult.

Classical computers require millions of years for extremely large numbers.

E. Artificial Intelligence

Modern AI requires:

  • Massive datasets
  • Huge GPUs
  • High electricity
  • Long training time
Example

Training GPT models requires:

  • Thousands of GPUs
  • Millions of watts
  • Weeks of computation
F. Optimization Problems
Examples
  • Airline scheduling
  • Delivery routing
  • Traffic control
  • Stock optimization
  • Supply chains

These require searching billions of combinations.

1.5 Energy Consumption

Modern data centers consume enormous electricity.

Example

AI models require:

  • Thousands of GPUs
  • Cooling systems
  • Huge energy bills

Future computing needs more efficient approaches.

1.6 Memory Limitation

Large simulations require:

  • TBs
  • PBs
  • Even Exabytes

Memory quickly becomes insufficient.

1.7 Parallel Computing is Not Enough

Supercomputers use:

  • Thousands of CPUs
  • Millions of cores

Even then,

Certain problems remain computationally infeasible.

1.8 Summary

Classical computing struggles with:

  • Optimization
  • Quantum simulations
  • Cryptography
  • Large-scale AI
  • Exponential search problems

Quantum computing aims to address many of these challenges.

Chapter 2: Quantum Computing Fundamentals

2.1 What is Quantum Computing?

Quantum computing is a new computing paradigm based on the principles of quantum mechanics .

Instead of classical bits, it uses qubits .

2.2 Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics describes the behavior of:

  • Electrons
  • Photons
  • Atoms
  • Molecules

At microscopic scales, particles exhibit behaviors unlike classical objects.

2.3 Basic Principles

Quantum computing relies on:

  • Superposition
  • Entanglement
  • Interference
  • Measurement

These principles enable new ways of processing information.

2.4 Quantum vs Classical Computing

2.5 Quantum Gates

Quantum gates manipulate qubits.

Examples:
  • X Gate
  • Y Gate
  • Z Gate
  • Hadamard Gate
  • CNOT Gate
  • Phase Gate

Unlike classical gates, quantum gates are reversible and represented by unitary matrices.

2.6 Quantum Circuit

A quantum algorithm is represented as a quantum circuit.

Example

               |
|0> -----------X------

This creates an entangled Bell state.

Chapter 3: Qubits

3.1 What is a Qubit?

A qubit (quantum bit) is the basic unit of quantum information.

Unlike a classical bit:

Bit
0
or
1

A qubit can exist in a superposition of both states.

3.2 Mathematical Representation

A qubit is written as: α|0> + β|1>

where:

  • α and β are complex probability amplitudes.
  • |α|^2 + |β|^2 = 1 .

3.3 Physical Implementations

Qubits can be built using:

  • Superconducting circuits
  • Trapped ions
  • Photons
  • Neutral atoms
  • Quantum dots
  • Spin qubits

Each platform has different strengths and challenges.

3.4 Bloch Sphere

A single qubit state can be visualized on the Bloch sphere.

  • North Pole → |0>
  • South Pole → |1>
  • Any point on the sphere → valid quantum state

This provides an intuitive picture of qubit rotations.

Chapter 4: Superposition

4.1 Definition

Superposition means a qubit can exist in a combination of basis states until measured.

Example

1/√2 (|0> + |1>)

This state has equal probability of yielding 0 or 1 upon measurement.

4.2 Hadamard Gate

Applying a Hadamard gate to |0> :

1/√2 (|0> + |1>)

This creates a superposition.

4.3 Why Superposition Matters

With n qubits, the state space has 2 n amplitudes.

Examples:
  • n=1 → 2 states
  • n=2 → 4 states
  • n=3 → 8 states

Quantum algorithms exploit this large state space through carefully designed interference; it does not mean all problems automatically receive an exponential speedup.

Chapter 5: Entanglement

5.1 Definition

Entanglement is a uniquely quantum correlation between qubits.

The state of one qubit becomes correlated with the state of another, even when they are separated.

5.2 Bell State

One of the simplest entangled states:

1/√2 (|00> + |11>)

Measuring one qubit immediately determines the correlated outcome of the other, regardless of the distance between them. This does not allow faster-than-light communication.

5.3 Creating Entanglement 

|0> ----H------●------
               |
|0> -----------X------

Steps:

  1. Apply Hadamard to the first qubit.
  2. Apply CNOT using the first qubit as control.
  3. The result is an entangled Bell state.

5.4 Applications

  • Quantum teleportation
  • Superdense coding
  • Quantum cryptography
  • Quantum communication
  • Quantum algorithms
  • Quantum error correction

Chapter 6: Quantum Advantage

6.1 Definition

Quantum advantage refers to demonstrating a computational task where a quantum computer outperforms the best known classical methods under comparable conditions.

It does not mean quantum computers are faster for every task.

6.2 Why It Happens

Quantum algorithms can exploit:

  • Superposition
  • Entanglement
  • Interference

to reduce the number of computational steps for specific problems.

6.3 Famous Algorithms

Shor's Algorithm
  • Integer factorization
  • Threatens RSA encryption if implemented on large fault-tolerant quantum computers.
Grover's Algorithm
  • Database search
  • Quadratic speedup over classical exhaustive search.
Quantum Simulation

Efficiently simulating quantum systems such as molecules and materials is one of the most promising applications of quantum computing.

Quantum Machine Learning

Potential applications include:

  • Quantum kernels
  • Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC)
  • Quantum Neural Networks (QNN)
  • Quantum PCA

Many proposed advantages are still being actively researched.

6.4 Applications

Potential areas include:

  • Drug discovery
  • Material science
  • Financial modeling
  • Logistics optimization
  • Climate modeling
  • Machine learning
  • Quantum chemistry

6.5 Current Limitations

Today's quantum computers face challenges such as:

  • Noise
  • Decoherence
  • Limited qubit counts
  • Gate errors
  • Readout errors

These limitations define the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era.

Chapter 7: Real Quantum Hardware

7.1 What is Quantum Hardware?

Quantum hardware refers to physical systems that implement qubits and quantum gates.

Different technologies use different physical phenomena to encode quantum information.

7.2 Major Quantum Hardware Platforms

1. Superconducting Qubits
Examples:
  • IBM Quantum
  • Google Quantum
  • Rigetti
Advantages
  • Fast gate operations
  • Mature fabrication techniques
Challenges
  • Require dilution refrigerators (~10–20 mK)
  • Sensitive to noise
2. Trapped Ion Qubits
Examples:
  • IonQ
  • Quantinuum
Advantages
  • Very high gate fidelity
  • Long coherence times
Challenges
  • Slower gate operations
  • More difficult to scale
3. Photonic Quantum Computers
Examples:
  • Xanadu
  • PsiQuantum
Advantages
  • Operate at or near room temperature
  • Well suited for quantum communication
Challenges
  • Photon generation and detection remain technically demanding.
4. Neutral Atom Qubits
Examples:
  • QuEra
  • Pasqal
Advantages
  • Naturally scalable arrays
  • Strong interactions via Rydberg states
5. Silicon Spin Qubits
Examples:
  • Intel
  • Research laboratories worldwide

Potentially compatible with existing semiconductor manufacturing processes.

7.3 Important Hardware Metrics

Researchers evaluate quantum processors using:

  • Number of physical qubits
  • Coherence time
  • Gate fidelity
  • Readout fidelity
  • Connectivity
  • Error rates
  • Quantum Volume
  • CLOPS (Circuit Layer Operations Per Second)

7.4 Quantum Software Ecosystem

Common tools include:

  • Qiskit (IBM)
  • PennyLane (Xanadu)
  • Cirq (Google)
  • Amazon Braket
  • Q# (Microsoft)

These frameworks allow users to design, simulate, and execute quantum circuits on simulators or real quantum hardware.
