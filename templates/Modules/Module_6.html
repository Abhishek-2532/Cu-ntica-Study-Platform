1.1 Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Quantum Computing (QC) are two of the most revolutionary technologies of the 21st century.

Artificial Intelligence focuses on making machines intelligent.

Quantum Computing focuses on solving computational problems much faster than classical computers by exploiting quantum mechanics.

Initially, both fields developed separately, but today researchers are combining them to build Quantum Machine Learning (QML).

1.3 Evolution of Quantum Computing

Quantum Computing emerged from quantum mechanics.

Instead of bits,

Quantum computers use Qubits.

Important Milestones

  • 1980: Paul Benioff - First quantum computer model
  • 1982: Richard Feynman - Suggested quantum computers for simulating quantum systems.
  • 1985: David Deutsch - Universal Quantum Computer
  • 1994: Peter Shor - Shor's Algorithm - Can factor large numbers exponentially faster.
  • 1996: Lov Grover - Grover's Search Algorithm - Quadratic speedup.

2000–2015

Development of

  • Superconducting Qubits
  • Ion Traps
  • Quantum Gates

2019

Google announced Quantum Supremacy.

5.2 Technical Challenges

Hardware Limitations

  • Few qubits
  • Noise
  • Short coherence time

Data Encoding

Encoding classical data into qubits is expensive.

Error Correction

Quantum computers require sophisticated error-correction techniques.

Scalability

Large QML models require many logical qubits, which are not yet available.

Benchmarking

It is difficult to prove a practical quantum advantage over optimized classical ML methods.

Overall Module Summary

Module 6 explains why Artificial Intelligence and Quantum Computing are natural complements. AI has evolved from rule-based systems to deep learning, while Quantum Computing has progressed from theoretical models to cloud-accessible quantum processors. Their intersection has given rise to Quantum Machine Learning (QML) , where quantum algorithms are used to enhance or accelerate machine learning tasks.

At present, hybrid quantum-classical computing is the dominant approach because today's quantum hardware is limited by noise and qubit count. Research in QML is expanding rapidly across universities, technology companies, and startups, with applications in healthcare, finance, chemistry, optimization, cybersecurity, and materials science. Although significant challenges remain—such as error correction, data encoding, and hardware scalability—the field offers exciting opportunities for research, innovation, and careers. As quantum hardware matures, the integration of AI and quantum technologies is expected to play a major role in solving computational problems that are difficult or impossible for classical computers alone.
