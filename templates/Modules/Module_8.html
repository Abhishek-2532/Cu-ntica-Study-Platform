1.1 Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) have transformed many industries, from healthcare and finance to robotics and natural language processing. Traditional machine learning algorithms process data stored as bits , where each bit can take one of two values: 0 or 1 .

Quantum Computing introduces a fundamentally different computational model based on the principles of quantum mechanics . Instead of bits, quantum computers use qubits , which can exist in a combination of states simultaneously due to superposition and become correlated through entanglement .

Because classical and quantum computers store and manipulate information differently, classical datasets cannot be directly processed by quantum algorithms. Before applying any Quantum Machine Learning algorithm, the classical data must first be transformed into quantum states.

This transformation process is called Quantum Data Encoding .

In simple terms:

Quantum Data Encoding is the process of converting classical information into a quantum representation that can be processed by a quantum computer.

Without this step, quantum algorithms such as Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVM), Variational Quantum Circuits (VQC), or Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) cannot operate on classical datasets.

1.7 Characteristics of a Good Encoding Method

An effective quantum data encoding scheme should:

  • Represent the dataset accurately.
  • Use as few qubits as possible, since current quantum hardware has limited qubits.
  • Keep circuit depth low to reduce errors from noise and decoherence.
  • Preserve relationships between data points that are important for learning.
  • Scale efficiently as the number of features grows.

Choosing an encoding method often involves balancing these goals.

1.8 Challenges in Quantum Data Encoding

Although encoding is essential, it is also one of the most challenging aspects of Quantum Machine Learning.

Some common challenges include:

  • High-dimensional data
    Modern datasets may contain thousands of features, while current quantum devices provide only a limited number of reliable qubits.
  • State preparation cost
    Preparing certain quantum states, particularly for amplitude encoding, can require deep and complex circuits.
  • Noise
    Today's quantum computers are noisy (often referred to as Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices). Longer encoding circuits are generally more susceptible to errors.
  • Data normalization
    Some encoding methods, such as amplitude encoding, require normalized input vectors before they can be loaded into a quantum state.

2.1 Introduction

In classical machine learning, data is stored directly in computer memory as numbers, text, images, or binary values . Algorithms such as Linear Regression, Decision Trees, and Neural Networks can process these values directly.

Quantum computers, however, operate on quantum states rather than raw numerical values. They manipulate these states using quantum gates , allowing computations that exploit superposition, entanglement, and interference.

As a result, a quantum computer cannot directly interpret a classical dataset . Before a quantum algorithm can process the information, the classical data must be transformed into a quantum state.

This transformation is called Quantum Data Encoding (also known as Quantum State Preparation , Quantum Feature Encoding , or Quantum Data Loading in different contexts).

Definition:

Quantum Data Encoding is the process of converting classical information into quantum states by applying quantum gates, making the data suitable for quantum computation and quantum machine learning algorithms.

2.7 Properties of a Good Encoding Method

An ideal encoding strategy should satisfy several criteria.

  1. Efficient

    The circuit should use as few gates as possible.

    More gates generally mean

    • longer execution time,
    • greater susceptibility to noise,
    • higher computational cost.
  2. Scalable

    The method should remain practical as

    • the number of features increases,
    • the dataset grows.
  3. Expressive

    Different inputs should produce distinguishable quantum states.

    Poor expressibility may cause different data points to appear almost identical to the quantum model.

  4. Hardware-Friendly

    Current quantum devices are noisy.

    A practical encoding method should work within

    • limited qubit counts,
    • restricted connectivity,
    • finite coherence times.
  5. Information-Preserving

    Important patterns and relationships in the classical dataset should remain accessible after encoding.

    For example, nearby data points in feature space should ideally map to related quantum states when the learning task benefits from that structure.

2.8 Factors Affecting the Choice of Encoding

Several factors influence which encoding technique is appropriate.

  1. Dataset Type

    Binary datasets often suit Basis Encoding, whereas continuous-valued datasets are commonly encoded with Angle or Amplitude Encoding.

  2. Number of Features

    If the dataset contains thousands of features, using one qubit per feature may not be practical.

    Amplitude Encoding can reduce qubit requirements, but typically at the cost of more complex state preparation.

  3. Available Hardware

    Current quantum hardware offers only a limited number of high-quality qubits.

    Encoding methods requiring many qubits or deep circuits may not be feasible on today's NISQ devices.

  4. Target Algorithm

    Different quantum algorithms favor different encodings.

    For example:

    • Variational Quantum Circuits often use Angle Encoding.
    • Quantum Kernel methods rely on Feature Maps.
    • Deep variational architectures may benefit from Data Re-uploading.

2.11 Challenges in Quantum Data Encoding

Despite its importance, encoding remains one of the major bottlenecks in Quantum Machine Learning.

Some key challenges are:

  • State preparation complexity: Efficiently preparing arbitrary quantum states can require deep circuits.
  • Noise sensitivity: Longer encoding circuits accumulate more errors on NISQ hardware.
  • Qubit limitations: Many datasets have more features than available qubits.
  • Normalization requirements: Some methods, such as amplitude encoding, require normalized vectors.
  • Trade-offs: Methods that minimize qubit usage may increase circuit depth, while simpler methods may require more qubits.

These trade-offs motivate the study of multiple encoding techniques rather than relying on a single universal approach.

3.7 Quantum Bits (Qubits)

A qubit is fundamentally different.

It can exist in a superposition of both basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩ .

The quantum state is |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

where

  • α and β are complex numbers.
  • They are called probability amplitudes .

Probability

The probability of measuring |0⟩ is |α|² .

The probability of measuring |1⟩ is |β|² .

Since one of these outcomes must occur,

|α|² + |β|² = 1 .

This is called normalization .

3.8 Dirac (Bra-Ket) Notation

Quantum computing uses Dirac notation , introduced by physicist Paul Dirac.

It provides a compact way to represent vectors and operations in quantum mechanics.

Ket

A quantum state is written as a ket : |v⟩ (a column vector).

Examples: 

|0⟩ = [[1],[0]]
|1⟩ = [[0],[1]]
|ψ⟩ = [[α],[β]]

Bra

The conjugate transpose of a ket is called a bra : ⟨v| (a row vector).

If |ψ⟩ = [[α],[β]] ,

then ⟨ψ| = [α* β*] ,

where * denotes the complex conjugate.

3.12 Hilbert Space

A Hilbert Space is the mathematical space in which quantum states live.

It is a complex vector space equipped with an inner product.

For a system of n qubits, the Hilbert space has dimension 2 n .

Examples:

  • 1 qubit → 2-dimensional Hilbert space.
  • 2 qubits → 4-dimensional Hilbert space.
  • 3 qubits → 8-dimensional Hilbert space.

A general n -qubit state can be written as |ψ⟩ = Σ i=0 2 n -1 α i |i⟩ ,

where the amplitudes satisfy Σ i=0 2 n -1 |α i |² = 1 .

Hilbert space is the mathematical framework in which all quantum algorithms and encoding methods operate.

3.13 Tensor Product

The tensor product combines multiple quantum systems.

If |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ and |φ⟩ = γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩ ,

then |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ = (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) ⊗ (γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩) = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩ .

General Rule

If one vector has dimension D 1 and another has dimension D 2 ,

their tensor product has dimension D 1 D 2 .

This is why adding one qubit doubles the size of the state space.

3.15 Probability Amplitudes

The numbers α and β are probability amplitudes .

They are generally complex numbers .

Example |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + i(1/√2)|1⟩

Probability P(0) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 ; P(1) = |i/√2|² = (1/√2)(-i/√2) = 1/2 .

Notice 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 .

3.16 Normalization

Every valid quantum state must satisfy ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1 .

Example

Vector v = [3, 4]

Normalized Vector v_norm = [3/5, 4/5]

Normalization is especially important in Amplitude Encoding , where classical feature vectors must first be normalized before being loaded into a quantum state.

3.17 The Bloch Sphere

A single qubit can be visualized on the Bloch Sphere , a unit sphere where every point on the surface corresponds to a pure one-qubit state.

The general form of a single-qubit state is |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e iφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩ ,

where:

  • θ is the polar angle,
  • φ is the azimuthal angle.

Important points:

  • North Pole → |0⟩
  • South Pole → |1⟩
  • Equator → Equal superpositions with different phases

Quantum gates such as R x , R y , and R z rotate the state around the corresponding axes of the Bloch Sphere. This geometric picture is especially useful for understanding Angle Encoding .

3.18 Unitary Transformations

Quantum gates are represented by unitary matrices .

A matrix U is unitary if U † U = UU † = I ,

where:

  • U † is the conjugate transpose of U ,
  • I is the identity matrix.

Unitary operations preserve normalization and ensure that the total probability remains equal to one.

Examples of unitary gates include:

  • Hadamard (H)
  • Pauli-X, Y, Z
  • Rotation gates ( R x , R y , R z )
  • CNOT

5.1 Introduction

Basis Encoding is the simplest and most intuitive quantum data encoding technique. It represents classical binary data directly as quantum computational basis states .

Unlike Angle Encoding or Amplitude Encoding, Basis Encoding does not use rotation angles or probability amplitudes. Instead, each classical bit is mapped directly to a qubit.

For example, 

Classical Binary Number
1011
is represented by the quantum state
|1011⟩

This is called a computational basis state .

Since quantum computers naturally operate on computational basis states, Basis Encoding is one of the easiest methods to implement.

5.2 Motivation

Suppose we want to encode the binary number 

101

A classical computer stores it as 

1
0
1

A quantum computer begins with all qubits initialized to |0⟩ .

To represent 101 , we simply flip the qubits whose classical value is 1 . 

|000⟩ ───X─── |100⟩ ───I─── |100⟩ ───X─── |101⟩

Result |101⟩

No normalization, parameter optimization, or state preparation algorithm is required.

5.3 Computational Basis States

A single qubit has two basis states |0⟩, |1⟩ .

For two qubits |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩ .

For three qubits |000⟩, |001⟩, ..., |111⟩ .

There are 2 n possible basis states.

Every binary string corresponds to one computational basis state.

5.4 Mathematical Representation

Suppose x = b n-1 b n-2 ...b 1 b 0 , where b i ∈ {0, 1} .

Basis Encoding maps this vector to |ψ(x)⟩ = |b n-1 ⟩ ⊗ |b n-2 ⟩ ⊗ ... ⊗ |b 0 ⟩ = |b n-1 b n-2 ...b 0 ⟩ .

This mapping is one-to-one.

For example x = 101

becomes |1⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩ = |101⟩ .

No approximation is involved.

5.5 Basis Encoding Algorithm

The algorithm is straightforward.

  1. Step 1

    Read the binary vector.

    Example 1101

  2. Step 2

    Initialize qubits |0⟩ ⊗n .

    |0000⟩

  3. Step 3

    For every bit equal to 1,

    apply an X gate.

  4. Step 4

    Final state |b n-1 b n-2 ...b 0 ⟩ .

    |1101⟩

5.6 Why Do We Use the X Gate?

The Pauli-X gate acts like a classical NOT gate.

Its matrix is X = [[0,1],[1,0]] .

It transforms X|0⟩ = |1⟩ and X|1⟩ = |0⟩ .

Since all qubits start in |0⟩ ,

applying an X gate flips selected qubits to |1⟩ .

6.1 Introduction

One of the biggest challenges in Quantum Machine Learning (QML) is converting continuous classical data into a format that a quantum computer can process.

Unlike Basis Encoding , which works only with binary data, Angle Encoding can naturally represent continuous-valued features by using the rotation angles of quantum gates .

Instead of storing a number directly inside a qubit, the number is used as the rotation angle of a quantum gate.

For example, 

Classical feature
x = 1.57

Instead of writing 

1.57

inside the qubit,

we apply R y (1.57) to an initialized qubit.

The qubit rotates on the Bloch Sphere , creating a quantum state that represents the input feature.

This makes Angle Encoding one of the most widely used encoding methods in modern Quantum Machine Learning because it is:

  • Simple
  • Hardware efficient
  • Easy to implement
  • Well suited for noisy quantum computers (NISQ devices)

6.4 Mathematical Representation

Suppose x = [x₀, x₁, ..., x n-1 ] is the classical feature vector.

For each feature, x i ,

apply R y (x i ) to the corresponding qubit i , which is initialized in |0⟩ .

The overall encoded state is |ψ(x)⟩ = R y (x 0 )|0⟩ ⊗ R y (x 1 )|0⟩ ⊗ ... ⊗ R y (x n-1 )|0⟩ .

More generally, Angle Encoding may use R x , R y , R z , or combinations of these gates depending on the chosen circuit architecture.

6.5 Rotation Gates

Angle Encoding relies on single-qubit rotation gates .

The three most common gates are

Rotation about X-axis

R x (θ) = [[cos(θ/2), -i sin(θ/2)], [-i sin(θ/2), cos(θ/2)]]

Rotation about Y-axis

R y (θ) = [[cos(θ/2), -sin(θ/2)], [sin(θ/2), cos(θ/2)]]

Rotation about Z-axis

R z (θ) = [[e -iθ/2 , 0], [0, e iθ/2 ]]

Each gate rotates the qubit around a different axis of the Bloch Sphere.

6.6 Bloch Sphere Interpretation

Every single qubit can be represented as a point on the Bloch Sphere .

Initially,

the qubit is |0⟩ ,

located at the North Pole.

Applying R y (x) moves the state along the surface of the sphere.

π/2 Equal superposition (up to relative phases depending on the axis)
x cos(x/2)|0⟩ + sin(x/2)|1⟩

different classical values correspond to different locations on the Bloch Sphere.

6.9 Feature Scaling

Classical datasets often contain values with very different ranges.

Example

Feature Value
Age 25
Salary 500000
Height 170

Using these numbers directly as rotation angles would create unnecessarily large rotations.

Therefore,

Common approaches include:

  • Min-Max Scaling x' = (x - min(x)) / (max(x) - min(x))
  • Standardization x' = (x - μ) / σ

After scaling, values are often mapped into ranges such as: [0, π] or [0, 2π]

to make better use of rotation gates.

6.10 Why Is Feature Scaling Important?

Without scaling,

large values may produce rotations that differ by multiples of 2π , leading to periodic behavior and potentially reducing the distinguishability of inputs.

Example

1000
Rotation
Ry(1000)

Since rotation gates are periodic, R y (θ) = R y (θ + 2kπ) ,

the resulting state may not preserve meaningful differences between features.

Scaled features improve numerical stability and often make training easier.

Angle Encoding is one of the most practical and widely adopted methods for loading classical data into quantum circuits. By mapping numerical features to the rotation angles of single-qubit gates, it provides an efficient and hardware-friendly approach to preparing quantum states for machine learning tasks. Its simplicity, shallow circuits, and compatibility with variational algorithms have made it the standard choice for many Quantum Neural Networks and Variational Quantum Classifiers. However, because it often requires one qubit per feature and does not by itself encode interactions between features, it is commonly combined with entangling gates or repeated encoding layers. In the next chapter, you will explore Amplitude Encoding , which takes a different approach by storing data directly in the probability amplitudes of quantum states, allowing exponentially more information to be represented with fewer qubits.

7.1 Introduction

One of the biggest limitations of Basis Encoding and Angle Encoding is the number of qubits required.

  • Basis Encoding requires one qubit for every binary bit.
  • Angle Encoding often requires one qubit for every feature.

Current quantum computers have only a limited number of high-quality qubits. This makes efficient use of qubits extremely important.

Amplitude Encoding addresses this challenge by storing information in the probability amplitudes of a quantum state instead of assigning one qubit to each feature.

This makes it one of the most qubit-efficient encoding methods available.

Definition:

Amplitude Encoding is a quantum data encoding technique in which the values of a normalized classical vector are stored in the probability amplitudes of a quantum state.

7.2 Motivation

Suppose we have a dataset with 1024 features .

  • Angle Encoding
    Needs approximately 1024 qubits
  • Basis Encoding
    Also requires approximately 1024 qubits
    (for a direct binary-feature representation)
  • Amplitude Encoding
    A quantum state with n qubits has 2 n amplitudes.
    Since 2 10 = 1024 ,
    only 10 qubits are theoretically sufficient to represent a normalized vector of length 1024.

This exponential compression is one of the main reasons Amplitude Encoding is so attractive in quantum algorithms.

Qubits Basis States Maximum Number of Real Amplitudes (up to normalization)
1 2 2
2 4 4

7.3 The Basic Idea

A quantum state of n qubits can be written as |ψ⟩ = Σ i=0 2 n -1 α i |i⟩ ,

where

  • α i are complex probability amplitudes,
  • the amplitudes satisfy Σ i |α i |² = 1 .

Amplitude Encoding stores the classical data directly in these amplitudes.

Example 

Classical vector
x = [x₀, x₁, x₂, x₃]

Instead of assigning one value to each qubit,

Amplitude Encoding prepares the state |ψ(x)⟩ = x₀_norm|00⟩ + x₁_norm|01⟩ + x₂_norm|10⟩ + x₃_norm|11⟩ ,

where x_norm = x / ||x|| .

Only two qubits are needed because there are 2² = 4 computational basis states.

7.4 Mathematical Representation

Suppose the classical vector is x = [x₀, x₁, ..., x M-1 ] ,

where M = 2 n for n qubits.

The first step is normalization .

Compute the norm ||x|| = √(Σ i=0 M-1 x i ²) .

The normalized vector is x_norm = x / ||x|| .

The encoded quantum state becomes |ψ(x)⟩ = Σ i=0 M-1 (x i / ||x||) |i⟩ .

7.5 Why Is Normalization Necessary?

Quantum states must satisfy Σ i |α i |² = 1 .

If the input vector is v = [1, 2] ,

then |v|² = 1² + 2² = 5 ,

which is not normalized.

Norm ||v|| = √5 .

Normalized vector v_norm = [1/√5, 2/√5] .

The encoded quantum state is |ψ⟩ = (1/√5)|0⟩ + (2/√5)|1⟩ .

Checking normalization: |1/√5|² + |2/√5|² = 1/5 + 4/5 = 1 .

7.6 State Preparation

Amplitude Encoding is often called Quantum State Preparation because the goal is to prepare a specific quantum state with desired amplitudes.

The workflow is: 

Classical Vector
       │
       ▼
Normalize
       │
       ▼
State Preparation Algorithm
       │
       ▼
Quantum State

Unlike Basis Encoding or Angle Encoding, preparing arbitrary amplitude-encoded states generally requires a dedicated state preparation algorithm rather than simply applying one gate per feature.

7.7 Example 1 

Vector
x = [1, 1]

Norm
||x|| = √(1² + 1²) = √2

Normalized vector
x_norm = [1/√2, 1/√2]

Quantum state
|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩

This is the same state obtained by applying a Hadamard gate to |0⟩ .

7.8 Example 2 

Vector
x = [0, 1, 0, 0]

Norm
||x|| = 1

Normalized vector
x_norm = [0, 1, 0, 0]

Quantum state
|ψ⟩ = 0|00⟩ + 1|01⟩ + 0|10⟩ + 0|11⟩ = |01⟩

7.9 Dimension of the Quantum State

For n qubits,

there are 2 n basis states.

This exponential growth allows Amplitude Encoding to represent very high-dimensional vectors using relatively few qubits.

7.10 Quantum Circuit

Unlike Angle Encoding,

there is no single universal circuit for arbitrary Amplitude Encoding.

Instead,

quantum state preparation algorithms construct a sequence of quantum gates whose combined action produces the desired amplitudes.

Conceptually, 

Classical Vector
↓
Normalization
↓
State Preparation
(Complex series of Ry, Rz, CNOT gates)
↓
Encoded Quantum State

The exact gate sequence depends on the chosen algorithm and the input vector.

7.11 Qiskit Implementation

Qiskit provides the initialize() instruction for state preparation. 

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
import numpy as np
vector = np.array([1,2,3,4], dtype=float)
vector = vector / np.linalg.norm(vector)
qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.initialize(vector, [0,1])
qc.draw("mpl")

Explanation

  • Convert the input to a NumPy array.
  • Normalize the vector.
  • Use initialize() to prepare the desired quantum state.

The circuit automatically generates the required state preparation operations.

Note: initialize() is convenient for simulations and demonstrations. Internally it decomposes into many elementary gates, so the resulting circuit can become deep for large input vectors.

7.12 PennyLane Implementation 

import pennylane as qml
import numpy as np
dev = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=2)
@qml.qnode(dev)
def circuit():
    vector = np.array([1,2,3,4], dtype=float)
    vector = vector/np.linalg.norm(vector)
    qml.AmplitudeEmbedding(
        vector,
        wires=[0,1],
        normalize=False
    )
    return qml.state()
print(circuit())

Since the vector is already normalized, normalize=False is appropriate. Alternatively, PennyLane can normalize the input automatically by setting normalize=True.

7.13 Advantages

  1. Extremely Qubit Efficient

    Stores 2 n values using n qubits.

  2. High Information Density

    Large vectors can be represented compactly.

  3. Ideal for Quantum Linear Algebra

    Many quantum algorithms assume inputs are already amplitude encoded.

  4. Useful for High-Dimensional Data

    Especially when qubit resources are limited.

7.14 Disadvantages

  1. Difficult State Preparation

    Preparing arbitrary amplitudes generally requires many quantum gates.

  2. Deep Circuits

    The state preparation circuit may be too deep for current noisy quantum hardware.

  3. Requires Normalization

    Input vectors must satisfy the normalization condition.

  4. Data Loading Bottleneck

    Efficiently loading classical data into quantum amplitudes remains an active research problem and is often called the input or data loading bottleneck in Quantum Machine Learning.

7.15 Computational Complexity

Suppose the vector length is M = 2 n .

Property Complexity
Qubits n = log₂M
State Preparation (general case) Approximately O(M) gates
Storage Efficiency Exponential in qubit count

The number of elementary gates required for arbitrary state preparation depends on the algorithm. General methods often require a number of gates that scales on the order of O(2 n ) , although more efficient constructions exist for structured data.

This illustrates the key trade-off:

  • Very few qubits are needed.
  • Preparing the state can be computationally expensive.

7.17 Applications

Amplitude Encoding is widely used in quantum algorithms that process vectors and matrices.

  • Quantum Principal Component Analysis (QPCA)
    Represents high-dimensional data efficiently.
  • HHL Algorithm
    Loads vectors for solving systems of linear equations.
  • Quantum Recommendation Systems
    Encodes user-item preference vectors.
  • Quantum Image Processing
    Represents pixel intensities as amplitudes after suitable preprocessing.
  • Quantum Chemistry
    Encodes molecular information for certain simulation algorithms.
  • Quantum Finance
    Represents financial time series and portfolio data.

7.18 Practical Considerations

When using Amplitude Encoding:

  • Always normalize the input vector before state preparation (or use framework support to normalize it).
  • If the vector length is not a power of two, pad it with zeros before encoding.
  • Be aware that arbitrary state preparation can produce deep circuits on current hardware.
  • Consider whether the qubit savings outweigh the cost of state preparation for your application.

7.19 When Should You Use Amplitude Encoding?

Amplitude Encoding is a good choice when:

  • The dataset has a very large number of features.
  • Qubit resources are limited.
  • The algorithm naturally operates on amplitude-encoded states (e.g., HHL or QPCA).
  • Simulations or future fault-tolerant hardware are available.

It may be less suitable for shallow NISQ circuits where state preparation dominates the computational cost.

7.20 Key Takeaways

  • Amplitude Encoding stores classical data in the probability amplitudes of a quantum state.
  • It is the most qubit-efficient among the common encoding methods.
  • Input vectors must be normalized before encoding.
  • Arbitrary state preparation is generally more complex than Basis or Angle Encoding.
  • The main trade-off is low qubit usage versus high circuit complexity .

Amplitude Encoding plays a central role in many quantum linear algebra and Quantum Machine Learning algorithms.

Chapter 7 Summary

Amplitude Encoding provides a powerful way to represent high-dimensional classical data using a remarkably small number of qubits by storing feature values in the amplitudes of quantum states. This exponential compression makes it especially attractive for algorithms such as HHL, Quantum Principal Component Analysis, and other quantum linear algebra techniques. However, the benefits come with a significant challenge: preparing arbitrary amplitude-encoded states often requires deep and complex circuits, making data loading one of the major bottlenecks for current quantum hardware. Understanding this balance between qubit efficiency and state preparation complexity is essential when deciding whether Amplitude Encoding is the right choice for a Quantum Machine Learning application.
