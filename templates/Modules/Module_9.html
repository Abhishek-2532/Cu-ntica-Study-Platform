1.1 Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has transformed numerous industries by enabling computers to perform tasks that traditionally required human intelligence. A major branch of AI is Machine Learning (ML) , where systems learn patterns from data instead of relying on explicitly programmed rules.

The intersection of these two fields has given rise to Quantum Machine Learning (QML) , which explores how quantum computing can enhance machine learning algorithms.

1.5 What is Quantum Computing?

Quantum Computing is a computing paradigm based on the principles of quantum mechanics.

Instead of bits, quantum computers use qubits .

A classical bit can only be:

  • 0
  • or
  • 1

A qubit can be:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

where:

  • α and β are complex probability amplitudes.
  • The probabilities satisfy: α ² + β ² = 1

Fundamental Quantum Properties

Superposition

A qubit can exist in multiple states simultaneously.

Example:

  • Classical
    • 0
    • or
    • 1
  • Quantum
    • 0 and 1 simultaneously
Entanglement

Two qubits can exhibit correlations that cannot be explained classically.

Example: 

Qubit A ────────┐
                │

                │

Interference

Quantum amplitudes can reinforce or cancel one another.

Constructive interference amplifies correct answers, while destructive interference suppresses incorrect ones.

1.9 The NISQ Era

Current quantum computers belong to the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era.

Characteristics:

  • Tens to a few thousand qubits (depending on the platform)
  • Noisy operations
  • Limited coherence times
  • Imperfect gates
  • No full error correction

Because of these limitations, deep quantum circuits are impractical.

Why Variational Algorithms?

Variational algorithms are well suited to NISQ devices because they:

  • Use relatively shallow circuits.
  • Combine quantum computation with classical optimization.
  • Can tolerate moderate hardware noise.
  • Are flexible for different tasks.

This is why Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs) are central to modern QML.

2.6 Vector Spaces and Hilbert Spaces

A vector space is a set of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

A Hilbert space is a vector space equipped with an inner product and complete with respect to the induced norm. In quantum computing, finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces (such as for qubits) are commonly used.

A qubit lives in a two-dimensional Hilbert space.

Two qubits occupy a four-dimensional Hilbert space.

Three qubits occupy an eight-dimensional Hilbert space.

Generally, n qubits occupy a 2 n -dimensional Hilbert space.

This exponential growth is one reason quantum systems are challenging to simulate classically.

Tensor products are fundamental for describing multi-qubit states and multi-qubit gates.

2.9 Probability Theory

Machine learning is based on probability.

For an event A, 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.

Properties

  • P(Ω) = 1 (sum of all probabilities)
  • P(∅) = 0 (impossible event)
  • P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)

Conditional Probability

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Bayes' Theorem

P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B)

Bayesian reasoning is used in probabilistic machine learning.

2.10 Quantum Probability

A qubit |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ must satisfy |α| ² + |β| ² = 1.

The probability of measuring |0⟩ is P(0) = |α| ² .

and the probability of measuring |1⟩ is P(1) = |β| ² .

This is known as the Born Rule .

2.11 Statistics

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])]

Corr(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y) / (σ X σ Y )

2.12 Information Theory

Information theory quantifies uncertainty and information.

Entropy (Classical)

For probabilities P = {p 1 , ..., p n }, S(P) = -Σ i p i log 2 p i .

Higher entropy means greater uncertainty.

Quantum Entropy

The von Neumann entropy generalizes classical entropy to quantum states: S(ρ) = -Tr(ρ log ρ), where ρ is the density matrix of the quantum system.

Applications include:

  • Quantum communication
  • Quantum cryptography
  • Quantum information processing

3.1 Introduction

Classical computers have transformed science and engineering by solving a vast range of computational problems. However, some problems—such as simulating quantum systems, solving certain optimization tasks, and factoring large integers—can become computationally expensive for classical computers.

Quantum computing offers a different computational model by exploiting principles of quantum mechanics. Instead of processing information using bits , quantum computers use qubits , which can exist in superposition and become entangled. These unique properties enable quantum algorithms that can outperform classical algorithms for specific problems.

Quantum Machine Learning combines these quantum capabilities with machine learning techniques to develop hybrid algorithms that may offer advantages for particular learning tasks.

3.3 The Qubit

The qubit is the fundamental unit of quantum information.

Unlike a classical bit, which stores only one of two values, a qubit stores a quantum state.

Computational Basis States

The two basis states are |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Any valid qubit state is a linear combination of these basis vectors.

Example

|ψ⟩ = (1/√2) (|0⟩ + |1⟩)

This represents an equal superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩.

3.4 The Bloch Sphere

Every pure single-qubit state can be represented as a point on the Bloch Sphere .

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e iφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩

where:

  • θ determines the latitude.
  • φ determines the longitude.

Important Points

State Position
|0⟩ North Pole
|1⟩ South Pole
|+⟩ Positive X-axis
|-⟩ Negative X-axis
|+i⟩ Positive Y-axis
|-i⟩ Negative Y-axis

The Bloch sphere provides an intuitive geometric picture of single-qubit states and rotations.

3.5 Superposition

Superposition allows a qubit to exist in a combination of basis states before measurement.

Example:

Hadamard gate on |0⟩ → (1/√2) (|0⟩ + |1⟩)

Measurement probabilities:

  • P(0) = 0.5
  • P(1) = 0.5

It is important to note that superposition does not mean the qubit simultaneously stores both classical values in a directly readable way . The quantum state evolves according to quantum mechanics, and measurement yields a single outcome according to the Born Rule.

3.6 Quantum Measurement

Measurement converts a quantum state into a classical outcome.

Suppose |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩.

Then

  • Probability of measuring 0: P(0) = |α| ²
  • Probability of measuring 1: P(1) = |β| ²

After the measurement, the state collapses to the observed basis state.

3.7 Multiple Qubits

For two qubits, |ψ⟩ AB = c 00 |00⟩ + c 01 |01⟩ + c 10 |10⟩ + c 11 |11⟩.

Possible basis states are |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩.

There are 2 2 = 4 possible computational basis states.

For three qubits, 2 3 = 8 basis states.

For n qubits, 2 n basis states.

This exponential growth is a defining feature of quantum state spaces.

3.8 Entanglement

Entanglement is a uniquely quantum correlation between systems that cannot, in general, be described as independent states.

Example (Bell State)

|Φ + ⟩ = (1/√2) (|00⟩ + |11⟩)

If one qubit is measured and found to be |0⟩, the other will also be |0⟩. If the first is measured as |1⟩, the second will also be |1⟩.

Entanglement is a key resource for:

  • Quantum communication
  • Quantum cryptography
  • Quantum teleportation
  • Quantum algorithms
  • Quantum machine learning

3.9 Quantum Interference

Quantum algorithms manipulate probability amplitudes , not probabilities directly.

Interference can:

  • Increase the amplitudes of desired outcomes (constructive interference).
  • Decrease the amplitudes of undesired outcomes (destructive interference).

This principle underlies algorithms such as Grover's search and the Quantum Fourier Transform.

3.10 Quantum Gates

Quantum gates are reversible operations represented by unitary matrices .

A matrix U is unitary if U † U = I, where U † is the conjugate transpose of U.

Identity Gate (I)

Leaves the qubit unchanged. 

I = [1 0]
    [0 1]

Pauli-X Gate

Acts like a classical NOT gate. 

X = [0 1]
    [1 0]

Transformations:

  • X|0⟩ = |1⟩
  • X|1⟩ = |0⟩

Pauli-Y Gate

Performs a bit and phase transformation. 

Y = [0 -i]
    [i  0]

Pauli-Z Gate

Leaves |0⟩ unchanged and changes the phase of |1⟩. 

Z = [1  0]
    [0 -1]

Hadamard Gate (H)

One of the most important gates in quantum computing. 

H = (1/√2) [1  1]
              [1 -1]

Creates superposition:

  • H|0⟩ = (1/√2) (|0⟩ + |1⟩) = |+⟩
  • H|1⟩ = (1/√2) (|0⟩ - |1⟩) = |-⟩

3.11 Rotation Gates

Parameterized rotation gates are central to variational quantum algorithms.

RX Gate

Rotation around the X-axis: 

R_X(θ) = [cos(θ/2)  -isin(θ/2)]
              [-isin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)]

RY Gate

Rotation around the Y-axis: 

R_Y(θ) = [cos(θ/2)  -sin(θ/2)]
              [sin(θ/2)   cos(θ/2)]

RZ Gate

Rotation around the Z-axis: 

R_Z(θ) = [e-iθ/2  0      ]
              [0         eiθ/2]

The angle θ becomes a trainable parameter in parameterized quantum circuits.

Common examples include:

3.14 Quantum Circuits

A quantum circuit is a sequence of quantum gates applied to qubits.

Example 

|0⟩ ──H────●────M
           │
|0⟩ ───────X────M

This circuit:

  • Initializes two qubits.
  • Applies a Hadamard gate.
  • Applies a CNOT gate.
  • Creates an entangled Bell state.
  • Measures both qubits.

4.1 Introduction

Parameterized Quantum Circuits (PQCs), also known as Variational Quantum Circuits (VQCs) or Parameterized Quantum Circuits (Ansätze) , are the backbone of modern Quantum Machine Learning and Variational Quantum Algorithms.

Unlike fixed quantum circuits, PQCs contain trainable parameters . These parameters are adjusted during training to optimize a chosen objective function, much like the weights in a classical neural network.

A PQC combines:

  • Quantum computing (to process quantum states)
  • Classical optimization (to update parameters)
  • Machine learning (to learn from data)

This hybrid approach makes PQCs suitable for current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices.

4.2 Why Parameterized Quantum Circuits?

Quantum computers available today are limited by:

  • Noise
  • Short coherence times
  • Gate errors
  • Limited qubit counts

Deep quantum circuits are difficult to execute reliably.

PQCs address these challenges by:

  • Using shallow circuits.
  • Optimizing a small number of parameters.
  • Leveraging classical optimizers.
  • Being adaptable to many applications.

They are widely used in:

  • Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC)
  • Quantum Neural Networks (QNN)
  • Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)
  • Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
  • Quantum Generative Models
  • Quantum Reinforcement Learning

4.10 Mathematical Representation

Suppose x is the input data and θ are trainable parameters.

The PQC implements U(θ) acting on an initial state: |ψ( x , θ )⟩ = U(θ)F( x )|0⟩ ⊗n .

The circuit output is typically an expectation value of an observable O :

E O ( x , θ ) = ⟨ψ( x , θ )| O |ψ( x , θ )⟩

This expectation value is used for prediction or optimization.

For regression: L( θ ) = Σ i (y i - E O ( x i , θ )) ²

6.1 Introduction

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) are among the most powerful supervised learning algorithms for classification and regression. They work by finding a decision boundary that maximizes the margin between different classes.

Many real-world datasets are not linearly separable in their original feature space. Classical SVMs address this using the kernel trick , which implicitly maps data into a higher-dimensional feature space where a linear separator may exist.

Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVMs) adopt the same idea but use quantum circuits to define and evaluate kernels. Instead of explicitly constructing the feature space, a quantum computer prepares quantum states whose overlaps define the kernel.

QSVMs are one of the earliest and most actively studied approaches in Quantum Machine Learning.

For a feature vector x , the decision boundary is w · x + b = 0.

8.1 Introduction

Kernel methods are among the most powerful techniques in machine learning. They enable algorithms to solve nonlinear problems by implicitly mapping data into higher-dimensional feature spaces, where linear models can often separate complex patterns.

Classical kernel methods have been highly successful in algorithms such as:

  • Support Vector Machines (SVMs)
  • Kernel Ridge Regression
  • Gaussian Processes
  • Spectral Clustering
  • Kernel Principal Component Analysis (KPCA)

Quantum computing extends this idea by encoding data into quantum states and defining kernels based on the similarity (overlap) of these states. This approach forms the basis of Quantum Kernel Methods , one of the most theoretically grounded areas of Quantum Machine Learning.

Unlike Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQCs) or Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs), quantum kernel methods generally do not require training quantum circuit parameters . Instead, they rely on carefully designed quantum feature maps .

8.2 What is a Kernel?

A kernel function measures the similarity between two data points.

Instead of explicitly transforming data into a higher-dimensional feature space, Φ(x), a kernel computes the inner product in that space directly.

Mathematically, K(x i , x j ) = Φ(x i ) T Φ(x j ), where

  • x i , x j = input samples
  • Φ = feature mapping

This approach is called the Kernel Trick .

**oooo****

A linear classifier cannot separate these points effectively.

After applying a suitable feature mapping,

oooooooooo

the data become linearly separable.

8.16 Quantum Kernel Estimation

Most common approach.

A quantum circuit estimates the overlap using an ancillary qubit.

9.1 Introduction

Modern machine learning often deals with high-dimensional datasets , where each sample may contain hundreds, thousands, or even millions of features. High dimensionality increases computational cost and can reduce model performance due to the curse of dimensionality .

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is one of the most widely used dimensionality reduction techniques. It transforms data into a smaller set of orthogonal variables called principal components , which retain most of the variation (information) in the original data.

Quantum computing offers a quantum analogue known as Quantum Principal Component Analysis (QPCA) . Instead of working directly with classical covariance matrices, QPCA analyzes quantum states represented by density matrices and uses quantum algorithms such as Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) to extract eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

9.2 What is Dimensionality Reduction?

Dimensionality reduction is the process of reducing the number of input features while preserving as much useful information as possible.

Example

Suppose a dataset has:

  • 1,000 features
  • 10,000 samples

Many features may be:

  • Highly correlated
  • Redundant
  • Noisy

Instead of using all 1,000 features, PCA may reduce the dataset to:

  • 20 principal components

that capture most of the variance.

Why Reduce Dimensions?

Benefits include:

  • Faster training
  • Lower memory usage
  • Noise reduction
  • Better visualization
  • Reduced overfitting
  • Improved interpretability

9.3 Curse of Dimensionality

As the number of features increases:

  • Distances between points become less informative.
  • Data become sparse.
  • Training becomes computationally expensive.
  • More samples are required to generalize well.

Example:

Features Search Space
2 Small
10 Larger
100 Very Large
1000 Extremely Large

Dimensionality reduction helps mitigate these issues.

9.5 Mathematical Foundation of PCA

Suppose X is an N × D data matrix.

where:

  • Rows represent samples.
  • Columns represent features.

Step 1 — Mean Centering

Subtract the mean of each feature: X centered = X - μ.

Step 2 — Covariance Matrix

The covariance matrix measures relationships between features.

Σ = (1 / (N-1)) X centered T X centered .

Step 3 — Eigenvalue Decomposition

Solve Σ ν = λ ν , where:

  • ν = eigenvector
  • λ = eigenvalue

Step 4 — Select Principal Components

Sort eigenvalues in descending order.

The eigenvectors associated with the largest eigenvalues define the principal components.

9.6 Principal Components

Suppose PCA finds:

Component Explained Variance
PC1 60%
PC2 25%
PC3 10%
PC4 5%

Using only PC1 and PC2 retains 85% of the total variance.

9.7 Limitations of Classical PCA

Although PCA is powerful, it has limitations:

  • Large covariance matrices become expensive to process.
  • Eigenvalue decomposition is computationally demanding for very large datasets.
  • PCA assumes linear relationships.
  • Classical implementations may become inefficient for extremely high-dimensional problems.

These limitations motivate research into quantum approaches.

9.8 Quantum Principal Component Analysis

Quantum PCA is a quantum algorithm that extracts the principal components of a density matrix rather than a classical covariance matrix.

Instead of classical eigenvalue decomposition, QPCA uses quantum operations to estimate eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

The algorithm is particularly relevant for quantum data (states already stored in quantum form). Loading arbitrary large classical datasets into quantum states efficiently remains a significant challenge.

9.9 Density Matrix

In quantum mechanics, a pure state is represented by |ψ⟩ ⟨ψ|, while mixed quantum states are represented using a density matrix ρ .

For a pure state, ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|

Example

If |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩ + |1⟩)

Then ρ = (1/2) (|0⟩⟨0| + |0⟩⟨1| + |1⟩⟨0| + |1⟩⟨1|)

Density matrices describe statistical mixtures and are central to QPCA.

9.10 Properties of Density Matrices

A valid density matrix ρ satisfies:

Hermitian

ρ = ρ †

Positive Semidefinite

All eigenvalues λ i satisfy λ i ≥ 0.

Unit Trace

Tr(ρ) = 1

These properties ensure valid quantum probabilities.

9.11 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Suppose ρ ν = λ ν , where:

  • ν = eigenvector
  • λ = eigenvalue

In QPCA,

  • eigenvectors correspond to principal components,
  • eigenvalues indicate their importance.

9.12 Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)

One of the key tools behind QPCA is Quantum Phase Estimation .

QPE estimates the eigenvalues (more precisely, phases related to eigenvalues of a unitary operator) associated with eigenvectors.

In many machine learning applications, datasets contain a large amount of redundant information. Instead of storing every feature, we can compress the data into a smaller representation while preserving the most important information.

A Classical Autoencoder is a neural network trained to reconstruct its input after compressing it into a lower-dimensional representation.

A Quantum Autoencoder (QAE) extends this idea to quantum information . Instead of compressing classical vectors, a QAE compresses quantum states into fewer qubits while preserving the essential quantum information.

10.2 What is an Autoencoder?

An autoencoder is an unsupervised learning model that learns to reconstruct its input.

It consists of two parts:

  • Encoder
  • Decoder

The encoder compresses the input into a latent representation.

The decoder reconstructs the original data from this compressed representation.

The objective is to make |ψ out ⟩ ≈ |ψ in ⟩.

The decoder reconstructs the original state.

10.11 Training Objective

Unlike supervised learning, Quantum Autoencoders are trained to maximize reconstruction quality.

The reconstructed state |ψ out ⟩ should closely match the original state |ψ in ⟩.

10.12 Fidelity

The most common objective uses quantum fidelity .

0 Completely different states
0.8 Good reconstruction

The goal is F → 1.

where L = 0 → perfect reconstruction → optimal solution.

Some implementations instead optimize observables associated with the trash qubits, which can be more practical experimentally.
